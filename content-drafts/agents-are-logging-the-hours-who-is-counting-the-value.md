# Agents Are Logging the Hours. Who Is Counting the Value?

AI agents have become remarkably good at producing evidence of activity. They run for hours, open tickets, write code, draft documents, and close loops that once waited in someone's queue. A dashboard can show all of it rising.

None of that tells a leadership team whether the business is better off.

Three publications from the past six weeks make the gap hard to ignore. Together they suggest that the scarce capability is no longer getting agents to work. It is knowing what their work is worth.

## Activity has become the easy number

In October, Anthropic published a detailed study of how work is changing inside its own company. By August, interactive agent hours outnumbered employee hours six to one, on a comparison that assumes a 40-hour human work week. Code commits per engineer had reached five times their level of six months earlier.

Those are striking figures from an unusually AI-intensive company, and the authors are careful about what they prove. "More code does not necessarily mean better code or business impact," they write. They tested one quality signal and found agent-written lines were removed or reverted at the same rate as human-written lines. They also caution that engineers who adopted agents were already on a steeper trajectory, and that AI errors may be harder to identify than human ones.

That discipline is the lesson. A company with exceptional access to the technology still treats output as a question to investigate, not a result to announce.

## Deployment is outrunning proof

The wider enterprise picture points the same way. Genpact's *Autonomy in Practice* research, published on November 12 and based on a self-reported survey of 2,005 senior decision makers across 17 industries, found that 79% of enterprises have AI agents in production. Only 20% use them broadly and consistently across functions. Only 15% have fully tied AI investment to profit-and-loss outcomes. On average, 49% of AI initiatives funded in the past 24 months delivered the value expected.

Genpact sells transformation services, so its conclusions deserve the same scrutiny as any vendor research. The pattern is still useful: being live is not the same as being accountable for a result.

Meanwhile, the supply of agents keeps growing. On November 10, IBM and Microsoft announced Imagine Enterprise, a platform to build, run, and govern agents, drawing on more than 6,000 pre-built agents and assets and aimed initially at financial services, insurance, and retail.

Platforms like this can make controls and observability easier to adopt. They cannot decide what a particular agent is supposed to be worth to a particular business. That decision stays with the organisation.

## Keep a value ledger for every agent

Before an agent scales, give it a simple ledger. It does not need to be elaborate. It needs five entries that someone is willing to sign.

**1. The baseline.** Record how the work performs today: cycle time, cost per case, error rate, backlog, or customer outcome. Without a baseline, every later improvement is an impression.

**2. The unit of value.** Name the result the agent exists to improve, and express it in the language of the business. Resolved claims without rework is a unit of value. Messages sent is not. Reconciled invoices is. Tasks attempted is not.

**3. The accountable owner.** Assign one named person who owns the outcome, not the technology. This person decides whether the agent is earning its place and has the authority to change or stop it.

**4. The full cost of the work.** Count more than licences and usage. Include human review, exception handling, rework, integration upkeep, monitoring, and the time experts spend correcting what the agent gets wrong. Work moved from one team to another's review queue has not disappeared.

**5. The quality and stop rule.** Define what must stay true while volume rises, and what happens if it does not. Set the threshold for pausing, narrowing, or retiring the agent before launch, while the decision is still calm.

Take an agent that prepares responses for a service team. Its activity report might show thousands of drafts. Its ledger asks better questions. Did time to resolution fall? Did reopened cases stay flat? How long did staff spend checking each draft? Who decides if complaint rates move the wrong way?

Those answers can justify expansion. They can also show that a promising agent needs a narrower role, or that the process around it should change first.

## Review value on a schedule

A ledger only matters if it is used. Review each production agent on a fixed rhythm, with the accountable owner in the room, and end every review with one of four decisions: expand, keep, redesign, or retire.

Retirement should be an ordinary outcome. Agents are easy to start and easy to forget, and an unreviewed agent keeps consuming budget, access, and attention long after its original case has faded.

This rhythm also protects good work. When an agent is genuinely producing value, the evidence makes the case for further investment far stronger than a chart of rising activity ever could.

## Measure the work, not the worker

It is tempting to manage agents like tireless employees and celebrate their hours. But hours were only ever a proxy for value, and a weak one. Agents make that proxy weaker still, because they can multiply activity at almost no visible effort.

The organisations that benefit most will not be those with the largest agent count. They will be those that can say, for each agent, what it was meant to improve, what it really costs, who answers for it, and when it should stop.

If you cannot complete that sentence, the agent is not ready to scale.
